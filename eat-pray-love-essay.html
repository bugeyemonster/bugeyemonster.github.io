The Absurdity of Seeking Meaning in Eat Pray Love

An existentialist reading of the film through Albert Camus’s concept of the absurd.

The 2010 film Eat Pray Love, directed by Ryan Murphy and adapted from Elizabeth Gilbert’s memoir, is routinely categorized as a conventional romantic drama and an inspirational travelogue. Viewed through the philosophical lens of Albert Camus’s concept of the absurd, however, the narrative reveals a starkly existential core. Camus defines the absurd as the fundamental confrontation between the human desire for inherent meaning, order, and purpose, and the cold, silent, unreasonable universe that offers none. Liz Gilbert’s year-long journey across Italy, India, and Indonesia is not merely a bourgeois sabbatical; it is a sustained collision with the absurd. Through her systematic dismantling of prescribed social roles, her confrontation with existential silence in an ashram, and her eventual acceptance of transient, fragile happiness in Bali, the film illustrates that meaning is never discovered in external geography or institutionalized belief—it is arbitrarily constructed in the face of an indifferent reality.

At the outset of the film, Liz Gilbert occupies a life that satisfies every conventional metric of Western fulfillment: a successful career as a writer, a brownstone in New York, a devoted husband, and a tight-knit social circle. Yet, she experiences an acute existential rupture. Praying on her bathroom floor in the middle of the night, Liz admits, “I don't want to be married anymore. I don't want to live in this house. I don't want to have a baby.” This moment exemplifies what Camus describes in The Myth of Sisyphus as the sudden collapse of the stage-sets of daily routine, where the “why” arises and everything begins in weariness tinged with amazement. The absurdity here lies in the friction between societal scripts and subjective consciousness. Liz has followed the formula designed to guarantee happiness, only to discover that the formula produces suffocation. Her husband, Stephen, represents the adherence to those scripts without interrogation; when confronted with her unhappiness, he asks, “Why can't you just be happy with what we have?” His question underscores the absurd divide: he accepts the constructed narrative as objective truth, while Liz recognizes its arbitrary nature. By divorcing Stephen and subsequently ending a passionate, chaotic rebound with David—a relationship fueled by the illusion that intense emotion equals cosmic destiny—Liz rejects the two primary mechanisms people use to mask the absurd: domestic conformity and romantic obsession.

Her first destination, Italy, serves as an exploration of physical sensation as a direct rebuttal to existential dread. In Rome, Liz abandons productivity, guilt, and long-term planning, immersing herself in food and language. Critics often dismiss this section of the film as superficial hedonism, yet within an absurdist framework, sensory immediacy is a rational response to a meaningless universe. If there is no ultimate metaphysical reward awaiting human beings, then the present physical moment becomes the only verifiable reality. During a pivotal Thanksgiving dinner with her Roman friends, Liz reflects on the Italian concept of il dolce far niente—the sweetness of doing nothing. Her friend Giovanni explains that Americans work tirelessly to earn the right to rest, whereas Italians understand that enjoyment requires no justification. When Liz purchases larger jeans without shame, declaring, “I'm so tired of saying no, and waking up in the morning and recalling every single thing I ate the day before,” she is rejecting the absurd tyranny of self-denial in service of an abstract ideal. In Italy, reality is stripped down to flour, tomatoes, and syntax. The film uses the comedic exaggeration of Liz’s culinary indulgence to highlight a deeper truth: human beings invent complex moral anxieties around basic biological survival, manufacturing guilt in a universe that does not care what size jeans they wear.

However, sensory pleasure cannot permanently silence the human demand for metaphysical clarity, which leads Liz to the second phase of her journey: an ashram in India. Here, the film confronts the absurdity of institutionalized spirituality and the delusion of forced transcendence. Liz arrives in India expecting enlightenment to function like a transaction—if she meditates hard enough, wakes up early enough, and scrubs enough floors, the universe will grant her peace. Instead, she is met with mosquitoes, heat, mundane chores, and the relentless noise of her own thoughts. The film explicitly mocks the Western commodification of Eastern mysticism through Liz’s initial frustration with the ashram’s silence. Her breakthrough—or rather, her realization of the absence of a breakthrough—occurs through her interactions with Richard from Texas. Richard, a weathered pragmatist who has survived addiction and familial estrangement, acts as the film’s sharpest absurdist commentator. When Liz complains that she cannot quiet her mind during meditation, Richard tells her, “You're chasing after this thing, Groceries, like it's gonna fall out of the sky and hit you on the head.” He forces her to confront the reality that her spiritual quest is merely another ego-driven attempt to impose control over chaos.

The absurdity of seeking cosmic answers is further crystallized in the subplot involving Tulsi, a young Indian girl forced into an arranged marriage. Liz, operating from a Western individualistic perspective, views Tulsi’s fate as a tragedy and attempts to project her own desires for freedom onto the girl. Yet Tulsi accepts her reality with pragmatic grace, asking Liz to share memories of her own wedding so Tulsi can vicariously experience a celebration she will never have. The juxtaposition is jarring: Liz has spent thousands of dollars to escape a marriage she freely chose, while Tulsi is resigned to a marriage she never wanted, and both women find themselves trapped by the arbitrary cultural architectures into which they were born. When Liz finally achieves a moment of stillness on the roof of the ashram, forgiving herself for the dissolution of her marriage, it is not because a deity has intervened. Rather, she stops demanding that the universe validate her choices. As Richard advises her, “The only thing more unthinkable than leaving was staying; the only thing more unthinkable than staying was leaving. That's the adventure.” The absurd is not resolved; Liz simply stops fighting the fact that existence offers no risk-free, guaranteed path.

The final act in Bali brings the film’s absurdist themes into sharpest focus through the character of Ketut Liyer, an elderly Balinese medicine man, and Felipe, a divorced Brazilian businessman. Ketut embodies an acceptance of the absurd disguised as folk wisdom. He has lost his teeth, relies on a sketch of his own aura drawn years earlier, and dispenses advice that borders on tautology. When Liz asks him how to find balance, Ketut tells her, “Sometimes to lose balance for love is part of living a balanced life.” On the surface, this sounds like a greeting-card platitude; analyzed closely, it is an admission that coherence is impossible. A “balanced life” is a contradiction in terms if it requires losing balance. Ketut’s famous instruction to Liz—“Meditate. Smile. Even smile in your liver”—reduces spiritual practice to a physical facial contraction, stripping away theological weight and emphasizing behavioral defiance in the face of suffering.

The absurd reaches its comedic and philosophical peak when Liz attempts to help Wayan, a single mother and healer in Bali who is perpetually on the verge of homelessness. Liz raises $18,000 from her friends back home to buy Wayan a house, expecting a straightforward, heartwarming resolution. Instead, Wayan repeatedly delays the purchase, changes her demands, and navigates a labyrinth of local bureaucracy and superstition. Liz’s frustration boils over as she realizes that her Western savior narrative—the belief that money and good intentions linearly produce positive outcomes—is entirely incompatible with the chaotic, non-linear reality of Wayan’s world. Reality refuses to cooperate with Liz’s desire for a neat moral victory. Even altruism is revealed to be messy, misunderstood, and resistant to narrative closure.

Ultimately, Liz’s romance with Felipe serves as the final test of her newfound understanding of the absurd. Both characters have been scarred by divorce and are acutely aware that love is neither permanent nor guaranteed. When Felipe proposes that they spend their lives together, Liz initially panics and pulls away, terrified of losing the fragile equilibrium she has constructed. She recognizes that committing to Felipe means re-entering the arena of vulnerability, loss, and eventual death—the ultimate absurd certainties. Her decision to return to him in the final scene is not a regression into the naive romanticism she abandoned in New York. In New York, she expected marriage to save her from existential emptiness. In Bali, she accepts that Felipe cannot save her, that their relationship carries no cosmic insurance policy, and that love is an act of rebellion against isolation.

Viewed as a whole, Eat Pray Love subverts its own glossy aesthetic by consistently denying its protagonist the permanent transcendent answers she initially seeks. Italy offers temporary sensory distraction; India reveals that the divine is largely silence and self-confrontation; Bali demonstrates that wisdom is paradoxical and human relationships are inherently unstable. Liz Gilbert begins the film believing that her dissatisfaction is a problem to be solved by changing her external circumstances. By the end, she realizes that dissatisfaction, ambiguity, and impermanence are the baseline conditions of human existence. Like Camus’s Sisyphus, who finds contentment not because his boulder stays at the top of the mountain, but because he embraces the struggle of pushing it, Liz finds peace only when she abandons the illusion of a fixed destination. The absurdity of reality in Eat Pray Love is not that life is painful, but that human beings relentlessly demand order from a world governed by chance—and true liberation begins the moment that demand is surrendered.
