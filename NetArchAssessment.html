Network Architecture Security Playbook

A step-by-step guide for non-interactive assessments.

Phase 1: Preparation & Scoping

This initial phase is critical for setting the foundation for a successful assessment. Proper preparation ensures you understand the objectives, constraints, and key areas of focus before diving into technical details. It's about asking the right questions to define the "what" and "why" of the engagement.

  • ✓

    Define Clear Objectives & Scope

    Confirm with stakeholders the exact goals. Is it to find architectural flaws, assess compliance, or evaluate readiness for a new system? Clearly document what is in-scope (e.g., core network, 6 departments) and out-of-scope (e.g., endpoints, cloud infrastructure).

  • ✓

    Identify Key Stakeholders & Asset Owners

    Create a list of contacts for each department and the central network team. Identify who owns the critical assets and who can provide architectural context. This is vital for scheduling interviews.

  • ✓

    Establish Assessment Framework

    Choose a framework to guide your analysis. This could be based on NIST SP 800-53, ISO 27002, or a custom model focusing on defense-in-depth principles like segmentation, access control, and monitoring.

  • ✓

    Submit Initial Information Request

    Send a formal request for all available documentation, configurations, and previous security reports. Be specific about what you need (see Phase 2 for details).

Phase 2: Information Gathering

This phase involves collecting the raw data for your analysis. Since documentation is poor, you will rely heavily on interviews and direct configuration review. The goal is to build your own understanding of the network architecture, asset criticality, and data flows.

Even if poor, collect everything available. Look for inconsistencies and outdated information. Cross-reference with interview notes.

  • Network Diagrams: High-level and low-level. Note their age and accuracy.
  • Asset Inventories: What are the critical assets in each department?
  • Data Flow Diagrams: How is data supposed to move between departments?
  • Firewall Rulebases: Often a good source of truth for intended connectivity.
  • Nessus/Vulnerability Scans: Identify high-risk systems, open ports, and services that can inform your view of the network's attack surface.
  • Previous Pen Test Reports: What were the past findings? Have they been remediated?

Interviews are your primary tool for filling in the gaps from documentation. Ask open-ended questions to understand intent and operational reality.

Questions for Network Infrastructure Team:

  • "Can you walk me through the core network topology and how the departments connect?"
  • "What is the philosophy for segmentation between departments?"
  • "How is remote access managed? VPNs, jump boxes?"
  • "What monitoring and logging capabilities are in place at the core?"

Questions for Departmental Owners/Admins:

  • "What are the most critical systems in your network? What do they do?"
  • "What other departments or external services do your critical systems need to communicate with?"
  • "Can you describe the internal segmentation of your departmental network?"
  • "Who is responsible for patching and maintaining the systems here?"

This is where you find the ground truth. Manually review configs for routers, switches, and firewalls. Focus on security posture, not just functionality.

  • Firewalls/Routers: Review ACLs. Look for "any/any" rules, overly permissive rules, and lack of egress filtering. Check NAT/PAT configurations.
  • Switches: Check VLAN configurations. Are sensitive systems properly segregated? Look for insecure settings like disabled port security, lack of DHCP snooping, and open management interfaces.
  • General Hardening: Are administrative services (SSH, Telnet, SNMP) secured? Are default credentials changed? Is logging configured to send to a central SIEM?
  • Routing Protocols: Check for routing protocol authentication (e.g., OSPF, BGP). Is route filtering in place to prevent route leaks?

Phase 3: Analysis

In this phase, you synthesize the information gathered to identify architectural weaknesses. It's helpful to approach this from two perspectives: a top-down view of the overall architecture and a bottom-up view of the individual departmental networks.

Top-Down: Core Architecture Analysis

Focus on how the network is structured as a whole. How are trust boundaries enforced between the departments and between the internal network and the outside world?

Perimeter Security

Is there a clear perimeter with dedicated firewalls? Is ingress/egress filtering enforced? How is the DMZ architected?

Inter-Department Segmentation

Is traffic between departments inspected (firewalled) or just routed? The ideal is a zero-trust approach where all inter-VLAN traffic is denied by default.

Centralized Services

How are services like DNS, NTP, and Authentication (AD/RADIUS) architected? Are they resilient? Are they a single point of failure?

Redundancy & Resiliency

Are core components (routers, firewalls) redundant (e.g., using HSRP/VRRP)? What happens if a core link fails?

Bottom-Up: Departmental Network Analysis

Analyze each department's internal network as its own entity. A weakness in one department can compromise the entire organization if segmentation is poor.

Phase 4: Synthesis & Reporting

The final phase involves consolidating your findings into a coherent narrative. The report should be clear, concise, and actionable. Your audience ranges from technical staff to management, so structure it accordingly.

Report Structure

  • 1. Executive Summary: A high-level overview for management. Summarize the key architectural risks and their potential business impact. Avoid technical jargon.
  • 2. Scope & Methodology: Briefly describe what was assessed and the methods used (document review, interviews, config analysis). State the "non-interactive" constraint clearly.
  • 3. Strengths: Start on a positive note. What is the organization doing well? (e.g., "The use of redundant core routers provides good resiliency.")
  • 4. Findings & Recommendations: This is the core of the report. Group findings by theme (e.g., Segmentation, Access Control) or by risk level. Each finding should have:
    • A clear title.
    • A description of the weakness.
    • The associated risk and business impact.
    • A concrete, actionable recommendation.
  • 5. Appendices: Include supporting evidence, such as sanitized configuration snippets or a list of interviewees (with their permission).

Example Finding

Finding: Insufficient Inter-Departmental Segmentation

Observation: Network traffic between the Finance (Dept 2) and R&D (Dept 5) VLANs is not inspected by a firewall. The core router is configured to route traffic directly between these segments.

Risk: A compromise within the R&D network, which may have less stringent security controls, could directly pivot to critical financial systems. This lack of a trust boundary significantly increases the lateral movement attack surface.

Recommendation: Re-architect the core network to route all inter-departmental traffic through a stateful firewall. Implement a default-deny policy and create explicit rules to only allow required business traffic between departments.

Tools & Resources

While this is a non-interactive assessment, several open-source tools can significantly help you visualize, analyze, and manage the information you collect. These tools focus on processing the data you've been given, not on actively scanning the network.

draw.io (diagrams.net)

Use Case: Network Visualization.
Essential for creating your own network diagrams as you understand the architecture. Don't trust the provided diagrams; build your own version based on configs and interviews. This process often reveals architectural flaws.

Nipper

Use Case: Automated Configuration Auditing.
While not open-source for the full version, there are community editions or similar tools. Nipper can parse device configurations (firewalls, routers, switches) and automatically report on hardening issues, risky firewall rules, and vulnerabilities.

Batfish

Use Case: Network Configuration Analysis.
An advanced, powerful open-source tool. You feed it all your network device configurations, and it builds a model of the network. You can then query this model to find out things like "Can Host A talk to Host B?", "What would be the impact of this firewall rule change?", or find unused ACLs.

NetBox

Use Case: IPAM & DCIM.
While primarily an "Infrastructure Resource Modeling" tool, it can be invaluable for organizing your findings. You can model the network structure, IP ranges, VLANs, and devices as you discover them. This creates a structured inventory that can be a valuable deliverable for the client.

Obsidian / Joplin

Use Case: Note Taking & Knowledge Management.
Use a powerful note-taking app to link your interview notes, configuration snippets, and findings together. Being able to quickly cross-reference information is key to identifying inconsistencies.
