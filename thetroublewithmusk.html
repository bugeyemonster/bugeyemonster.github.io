Gambling with Eternity

"The only thing I know is that I know nothing." — Socrates

A modern trend has emerged among the ultra-wealthy and the intellectual elite: Longtermism. It is the belief that because the future could contain trillions of humans living across millions of years, the most ethical action today is whatever maximizes the "expected value" of that distant future.

However, this philosophy rests on a precarious assumption: that we possess the epistemic capability to predict what will be "good" for a civilization 1,000 or 1,000,000 years hence. This document argues that due to the chaotic nature of the universe and our limited knowledge, Longtermism often collapses into a dangerous gamble without feedback.

The Mathematical Model of Epistemic Decay

If we cannot predict the weather 30 days from now, how can we predict the "good" 300 years from now?

We can model the Confidence of Utility ($C_u$) over time ($t$) using a decay function:

C_u(t) = I_0 * e^(-kt)

Where $I_0$ is the initial impact and $k$ is the Entropy of Unforeseen Consequences.

As $t$ increases, the probability that your action has the intended effect approaches zero, and the probability of random (potentially catastrophic) noise increases.

The Logic of "Least Suffering"

The arguments presented here suggest a fundamental flaw in Longtermism: it attempts to optimize variables that do not yet exist.

The mathematical reality of epistemic decay forces a conclusion: The only way to ensure "Good" in a chaotic universe is to reduce "Bad" in the verifiable present. We know what suffering is. We know when it stops. We receive immediate feedback when we alleviate it.

To ignore the crying child at your feet for the sake of a theoretical trillion humans in the stars is not just callous; mathematically, it is likely a calculation error.
